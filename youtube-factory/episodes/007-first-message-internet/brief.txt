VIDEO — "The First Message Ever Sent on the Internet Crashed After Two Letters"

SCENE 1
NARRATION: At about ten-thirty on the night of October twenty-ninth, nineteen sixty-nine, a graduate student at UCLA sat down at a computer terminal and began to type one word: L-O-G-I-N. At the other end of the line, three hundred and fifty miles away at the Stanford Research Institute, a researcher held a telephone to his ear. "Did you get the L?" "Got the L." "Did you get the O?" "Got the O." "Did you get the G?" And then, silence. The computer had crashed.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: October 29, 1969
VISUAL: a dark lab; a terminal; letters typing L, O, then a glitch; a phone call bubble; a map line between Los Angeles and Stanford with "~350 miles".

SCENE 2
NARRATION: That crash, two letters into the very first message, is the birth of the internet. And the strangest part of this story is that almost nobody who built it imagined what it would become.
VISUAL: two letters glowing, then a network of lines spreading outward from them.

SCENE 3
NARRATION: To understand how they got there, go back to nineteen sixty-six, to a government office at the Pentagon. A man named Bob Taylor ran a research program for the military's advanced research agency. In his office sat three computer terminals. Each was connected to a different computer, in a different city. And each spoke a different language.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1966 · Three terminals
VISUAL: an office with three terminals, each labelled with a different city and a different command style.

SCENE 4
NARRATION: To talk to all three, Taylor had to switch chairs, log in separately, and remember three sets of commands. It struck him as ridiculous. Why shouldn't all these machines be able to talk to each other? Reportedly, he pitched the idea to his boss, and had about a million dollars approved in twenty minutes.
VISUAL: a man swivelling between three chairs; the three terminals joined by one glowing line; a stopwatch showing 20 minutes and a $1M tag.

SCENE 5
NARRATION: You'll often hear that the internet was built to survive a nuclear war. It's a popular story. But the people who built the network say that wasn't the goal. The goal was much simpler: expensive computers were scattered across the country, and researchers wanted to share them.
VISUAL: a mushroom cloud icon crossed out; scattered computers on a map linked by lines; the words "sharing, not survival".

SCENE 6
NARRATION: The big technical idea was something called packet switching. Instead of holding a line open, like a phone call, you break a message into small pieces, called packets. Each one finds its own way across the network, and they're put back together at the other end. Imagine mailing a book by tearing it into postcards.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: Packet switching
VISUAL: a book tearing into postcards; each taking a different route across a map; reassembling into the book.

SCENE 7
NARRATION: The idea came from more than one place. Paul Baran in the United States and Donald Davies in Britain both worked it out, independently. And the giant telephone company of the time, AT&T, reportedly told Baran it would never work. Phone lines, they said, were meant for calls.
VISUAL: two name cards (Baran, Davies) with flags; a phone company logo with a "won't work" stamp.

SCENE 8
NARRATION: A small Boston firm, Bolt Beranek and Newman, won the contract to build the machines that would sit at each site. Called Interface Message Processors, each was about the size of a refrigerator. Their job was to be the middlemen, receiving packets and passing them along.
VISUAL: a refrigerator-sized machine labelled "IMP" with arrows entering and leaving; a small BBN tag.

SCENE 9
NARRATION: The first one arrived at UCLA in the autumn of nineteen sixty-nine, in the lab of a professor named Leonard Kleinrock. The second went to the Stanford Research Institute. Two machines, three hundred and fifty miles apart, joined by a leased telephone line. And a graduate student named Charley Kline, with a very short message to send.
VISUAL: an IMP arriving in a lab; a leased line stretching up the coast; Kleinrock's and Kline's name cards.

SCENE 10
NARRATION: After the crash, the team found the bug and fixed it. About an hour later, they tried again. This time, the whole word went through: L-O-G-I-N. The first message had failed. The second succeeded. And the network was alive.
VISUAL: a retry button; a progress bar; the full word LOGIN typing out; a green check.

SCENE 11
NARRATION: Years later, Kleinrock pointed out something that made people smile. Those first two letters, L and O, could be read as the beginning of a phrase: lo and behold. By accident, the very first message on the internet was a kind of announcement. Short. Fragile. And somehow, exactly right.
VISUAL: the letters L-O bursting into "Lo and behold"; a faint smile from a lab figure.

SCENE 12
NARRATION: By December of nineteen sixty-nine, the network had four computers. UCLA. The Stanford Research Institute. UC Santa Barbara. And the University of Utah. Four machines in four labs. That was the entire internet.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: December 1969 · 4 computers
VISUAL: a map of the western United States with four glowing dots connecting one by one.

SCENE 13
NARRATION: Then something happened that nobody had planned. In nineteen seventy-one, an engineer named Ray Tomlinson sent a message from one computer to another, and picked a symbol to separate the user's name from the machine's name. The at sign. He had just invented email.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1971 · The first email
VISUAL: a name, then an "@" popping in, then a machine name; an envelope icon flying between two computers.

SCENE 14
NARRATION: Within a few years, email was most of the traffic on the network. The designers had built a system for sharing expensive computers. What people actually wanted was to talk to each other. It's a pattern that would repeat again and again in the story of the internet.
VISUAL: a traffic bar: "computing" tiny, "email" huge; people icons chatting across the network.

SCENE 15
NARRATION: But there was a new problem. Other networks were being built, and they couldn't talk to each other. In the mid-nineteen-seventies, two engineers, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, designed a common language that every network could speak. It was called TCP/IP. Think of it as a shared grammar for machines.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: TCP/IP
VISUAL: several separate networks with different shapes; a common language layer sliding over them; two name cards (Cerf, Kahn).

SCENE 16
NARRATION: On January first, nineteen eighty-three, the old ARPANET switched over to TCP/IP. Many people call that day the true birth of the internet: not one network, but a network of networks, all speaking the same language.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: January 1, 1983
VISUAL: a calendar flipping to January 1; networks snapping together into a mesh of networks.

SCENE 17
NARRATION: And still, the internet wasn't something most people could use. It was a road, without any shops. Then, in nineteen eighty-nine, a British scientist at the European research lab CERN, Tim Berners-Lee, proposed a way to link documents together. His boss, according to the story, wrote on the proposal: vague, but exciting.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1989 · "Vague, but exciting"
VISUAL: a road labelled "internet"; a proposal page with a handwritten note; linked documents appearing on the road.

SCENE 18
NARRATION: That idea became the World Wide Web. The first website went online in nineteen ninety-one. And in nineteen ninety-three, CERN made the technology free for anyone to use. That's the difference. The internet is the network. The web is something you use on it. One is the road. The other is the places you can go.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: 1993 · Free for everyone
VISUAL: a road and a map of destinations; a "royalty-free" stamp; a browser window opening.

SCENE 19
NARRATION: Today, more than five billion people are online. They send messages to each other every second, in every language, across every ocean. All of it descends from a single fragile experiment, in which a student typed one word, and the machine died after two letters.
ON-SCREEN TEXT: More than 5 billion people
VISUAL: a world map lighting up with connections; a counter rising past five billion; two letters in the corner.

SCENE 20
NARRATION: And here's what makes the story so human. The people who built it were not trying to change the world. They were trying to solve a tiny problem: three terminals, three languages, one frustrated man. The first message failed. They fixed it, and they tried again.
VISUAL: Taylor's three terminals returning; a retry arrow; "fix, then try again" in large type.

SCENE 21
NARRATION: Remember those two letters. L. O. A crash, and a promise. The best inventions rarely announce themselves with a bang. They begin as something small, and a little broken. Which is exactly why so many people miss them.
VISUAL: the opening dark lab; the letters L and O glowing in the dark.

SCENE 22
NARRATION: Next time: two students tried to sell their idea for one million dollars. The buyer said no. That idea became Google.
VISUAL: two student silhouettes holding a paper; a "$1M" tag; a buyer waving no; end card: channel name.
